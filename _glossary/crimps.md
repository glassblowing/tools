## What it is

Crimps are tweezers or [jacks](/glossary/jacks/) whose ends carry shaped plates instead of points. Murano's glass museum lists *rigadin* crimps among the forms of *borsella*, giving "an embossing effect like the ribbing on leaves, the shape of flower petals etc."{% include cite.html n=1 %}

## How it's used

Squeezed on hot glass, the plates press their pattern into it. The Conciatore lexicon describes many traditional Murano crimps by what they make:{% include cite.html n=2 %}

- *Borsee a rigadin*, fine-ribbed or "straight line" crimps, with fine, shallow, close-set grooves.
- *Borsee a gatolo*, ribbed crimps, named for the number of grooves (*gatoi*) on the plates.
- *Borsee a spina de pese*, "fish-bone" crimps, now mostly used for leaves.
- *Borsee da capar*, scallop crimps, for wavy edges and lips.
- *Borsee a geiosia*, crimps with a crossed pattern, named after the Venetian lattice window screen.
- *Borsee da oci*, eye crimps, with two short cylinders for pressing in the eyes of small animals on goblet stems.
- *Borsee a squeoto* or *a scugier*, spoon crimps, with two nesting half-spheres.

Carlo Donà's Murano catalog shows how varied the family is, with crimps for prunts, *morise*, eyes, lines, rose petals, leaves, and chandelier leaves.{% include cite.html n=3 %}

## History

The names above are Venetian. *Rigadin* comes from *riga*, "line";{% include cite.html n=2 %} *gelosia* (Venetian *geiosia*) was the woven screen put over windows, from *geloso*, "jealous".{% include cite.html n=2 %}
