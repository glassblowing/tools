## What it is

Jacks are two metal blades joined at the back by a spring, held like big tongs. The glassblower controls how far apart the blades are, and uses them on the glass while it turns on the pipe or punty. The Corning Museum of Glass also gives the older English name *pucellas*.

## How it's used

Almost every blown piece passes through the jacks:

- **Necking down:** squeezing the glass near the pipe with the inside edges of the blades to make the "jack line" where the piece will later break off. On Murano the common jacks are *borsee da siegar*, from the word for "to saw", after this sawing motion.
- **Shaping:** pressing the outside of the bubble straight or at an angle to model it.
- **Opening:** once a piece is on the punty, the blades go inside to open and flare the mouth.

## History

Murano's glass museum calls the *borsella*, together with the blowpipe, "the glass master's main and most versatile tool". The Conciatore lexicon notes that the word was used by goldsmiths for a spring clamp before glassmakers adopted it, and that jacks were traditionally made in two parts soldered together: blades forged by hand from iron, and a flat spring hammered cold around an anvil to give it its springiness. Today the blades come from pre-sized steel bars and the springs are often water-jet cut.

## Variations

- **Standard** jacks have straight, sharp-edged blades for necking and shaping.
- **Round** and **oval** jacks have rounder blades that open without leaving a sharp line.
- **Cup** and **goblet** jacks have finer blades for cup and stem work.
- Blades come in steel, stainless, or bronze; [parchoffi](/glossary/parchoffi/) swap them for wood.
