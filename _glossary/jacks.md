## What it is

Jacks are two metal arms joined at one end by a spring; the glassworker controls the distance between them.{% include cite.html n=3 %} Wikipedia describes them as shaped "somewhat like large tweezers with two blades".{% include cite.html n=4 %} The Corning Museum of Glass gives *borsella* and *pucellas* as other names for the tool.{% include cite.html n=3 %}

## How it's used

Jacks are used for many jobs while shaping the hot glass, including forming the mouths of open vessels.{% include cite.html n=3 %} The Conciatore lexicon lists the main ones:{% include cite.html n=2 %}

- **Necking down:** squeezing the glass near the blowpipe with the inside of the blades. On Murano the everyday jacks are *borsee da siegar*, from the word for "to saw", after this motion.
- **Widening and narrowing** the piece.
- **Opening** hollow pieces with the outside of the blades.
- **Modelling** the glass by pressing the hollow form straight on or at an angle.

## History

Murano's glass museum describes the *borsella* as flexible iron tongs used to shape molten glass, made in different forms for different jobs, and calls them, together with the blowpipe, "the glass master's main and most versatile tool".{% include cite.html n=1 %}

According to the Conciatore lexicon, the origin of the word is unknown, but goldsmiths used *borsela* for a spring clamp long before, and the glassmakers probably borrowed it.{% include cite.html n=2 %} Traditionally the jacks were made in two parts soldered together: the blades, forged by hand from iron, and the spring and handle, a flat bar bent by hammering it cold around an anvil, which gave it its spring. Today the blades come from pre-sized steel bar and the backs are often cut by water jet.{% include cite.html n=2 %}

## Variations

Blade shape and material vary: the jacks in this archive include standard, round, oval, cup and goblet blades in steel, stainless and bronze. [Parchoffi](/glossary/parchoffi/) replace the metal blades with wood.{% include cite.html n=2 %}
