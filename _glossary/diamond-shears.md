## What it is

Diamond shears have angled blades with the cutting edge on the inside. Partly open, the two blades frame a diamond-shaped hole, which is where the English name comes from; closing them squeezes the glass toward the center and cuts it from all sides. Most also have a small round notch at the tip.

## How it's used

They're for round cuts: cutting a gather off cleanly when an assistant brings it to the bench, cutting the end of a piece off in a round shape, or pressing a round groove into hot glass while it turns. Squeezed hard, they can crack a cold-enough piece free from the punty for sculpture.

The notch at the tip holds a pipe or punty. When an assistant brings a bit or bubble down onto a piece, the gaffer can catch the rod in the notch and steer it into place.

## History

On Murano they're *tagianti tonde*, literally "round shears", and the Czech name, *kulaté nůžky*, means the same thing; "diamond" is the English name. Murano's glass museum lists straight and round shears as the glassmaker's two main kinds of shears.

The Conciatore lexicon notes that in the United States some diamond shears are made with a fully round opening rather than a diamond, and some without the tip notch, and that some flameworkers prefer the round hole.

## What to look for

- **Blade size** (often given as the length of the cutting blades) sets how big a gather they'll cut.
- **Notch:** most have one for steering a pipe; check if you rely on it.
- **Steel and handles:** as with [straight shears](/glossary/straight-shears/), D2 or carbon steel, and standard or small handles.
