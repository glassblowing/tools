## What it is

Diamond shears have angled blades with the sharp edge on the inside, and are used for round cuts.{% include cite.html n=1 %} Their blades form a diamond shape when partly open,{% include cite.html n=3 %} which is where the English name comes from: the opening in the middle looks like a cut diamond.{% include cite.html n=2 %} Most also have a small round indentation at the end of the blades.{% include cite.html n=2 %}

## How it's used

Turning the piece while closing them, a glassblower can press a round valley into the glass, cut the end of a piece off in a round shape, or cut a gather an assistant brings to the bench into a neat round form.{% include cite.html n=2 %} Squeezed hard, they can free a sculptural piece from the punty where the colder glass breaks.{% include cite.html n=2 %} Wikipedia's summary is simply that they're "used for cutting off masses of glass".{% include cite.html n=3 %}

The indentation at the tip holds a pipe or punty: when an assistant brings a bubble or a gather down onto a piece, the master can hold the rod in it to guide it into place.{% include cite.html n=2 %}

## History

Murano's glass museum lists *tagianti tonde*, diamond shears, as one of the glassmaker's two kinds of shears, next to straight shears.{% include cite.html n=1 %} *Tonde* means "round"{% include cite.html n=2 %}, the same as the Czech name, *kulaté nůžky*, used by the Nový Bor shear maker Mitiska.{% include cite.html n=4 %}

Writing from the United States, the author of the Conciatore lexicon notes that some American diamond shears are made with a fully round opening instead of a diamond, some without the indentation at the tip, and that some flameworkers prefer the round hole.{% include cite.html n=2 %}
